# The Complete Type 1 Diabetes Management Manual
**mg/dL edition** — practical guidance for improving Time in Range and understanding glucose fluctuations

All glucose values in this manual are in **mg/dL**.

The objective is to maximise Time in Range while minimising hypoglycaemia, glucose variability, and the burden of daily diabetes management.

---

## 1. Glucose targets

For most non-pregnant adults with Type 1 diabetes:

| Metric | Target |
| --- | --- |
| Time in Range (70–180 mg/dL) | at least 70% of the day |
| Time below 70 mg/dL | <4% (~less than 58 minutes daily) |
| Time below 54 mg/dL | <1% (~less than 15 minutes daily) |
| Glucose variability (CV) | ≤36% (lower is generally preferable) |

### Glucose ranges at a glance

| Range (mg/dL) | Band |
| --- | --- |
| <54 | Very low |
| 54–69 | Low |
| 70–180 | In range |
| 181–250 | High |
| >250 | Very high |

This scale is a reference for bands, not recommended time spent in each range.

---

## 2. The glucose detective framework

When glucose is unexpectedly high or low, investigate:

1. **Timing** — After eating, overnight, during exercise, or while fasting?
2. **Insulin** — What basal and rapid-acting insulin was active?
3. **Food** — How many carbohydrates, and how quickly absorbed?
4. **Exercise** — Recent aerobic, resistance, or intense exercise?
5. **Lifestyle** — Sleep disruption, stress, illness, alcohol, dehydration?
6. **Repetition** — Isolated event or recurring pattern?

The most useful information is often not the glucose value itself, but what happened during the preceding **4–6 hours**, and sometimes longer after exercise.

## 3. Nutrition and glucose management

| Food characteristic | Glucose impact | What to investigate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fast-absorbing carbohydrates | Rapid rise, often within 30–90 minutes | Insulin timing, carbohydrate quantity |
| High-fibre carbohydrates | Often slower absorption | Total carbohydrates and delayed effects |
| High-fat meals | Can delay digestion and prolong elevation | Glucose 3–6 hours after eating |
| High-protein meals | Large amounts may cause a delayed rise | Gradual increases several hours later |
| Liquid carbohydrates | Often absorbed rapidly | Portion size and bolus timing |

### Illustrative meal response (same meal, different bolus timing)

| Time | Bolus at meal (mg/dL) | Earlier bolus (mg/dL) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 min | 108 | 108 |
| 30 min | 162 | 135 |
| 60 min | 225 | 162 |
| 90 min | 207 | 149 |
| 120 min | 162 | 130 |
| 180 min | 117 | 117 |

Conceptual curves only. Earlier bolusing is not always safer or appropriate, especially with Fiasp, falling glucose, or delayed food absorption.

**Key principle:** Correct carbohydrate counting does not guarantee good control if insulin action and food absorption are poorly synchronised.

For each recurring meal-related spike, examine carbohydrate estimation, bolus timing, insulin on board, and meal composition before assuming the dose was insufficient.

## 4. Insulin management

### Basal insulin

Basal insulin provides background coverage between meals and overnight.

| CGM pattern | What to investigate |
| --- | --- |
| Repeated overnight rise | Dawn phenomenon, basal coverage, evening food |
| Repeated overnight fall | Excess insulin effect, evening exercise, alcohol |
| Rise while fasting | Basal coverage, stress, illness, previous meals |
| Fall while fasting | Basal effect, active bolus, physical activity |

Long-acting insulin such as Tresiba works over an extended period, so a dose change may take several days to show its full effect. Avoid changing basal insulin in response to one isolated reading.

### Insulin-to-carbohydrate ratio (illustrative)

Example: 1 unit per 10 g carbohydrate → meal units = carbohydrates (g) / 10.  
For 60 g carbohydrate, the illustrative result is 6 units.

This is **not** a recommended personal dose. Individual ratios vary and may differ by time of day.

### Insulin sensitivity factor (illustrative)

Example: correction factor 36 mg/dL per unit, current glucose 216 mg/dL, target 126 mg/dL:

(216 − 126) / 36 = 2.5 units

This is only an unadjusted mathematical example. Actual correction dosing must account for active insulin, trend direction, exercise, illness, and the individual's prescribed correction plan.

**Avoid insulin stacking:** Additional insulin given before previous rapid-acting insulin has finished working can cause delayed hypoglycaemia.

### Insulin absorption

If insulin appears less effective than usual, check repeated injections into the same area, lipohypertrophy, leaking injections, storage problems, or missed doses.

**Never omit basal insulin in Type 1 diabetes**, including when not eating, unless following a specialist-supervised plan.

## 5. Exercise and glucose control

Exercise can cause both hyperglycaemia and hypoglycaemia, depending on intensity, duration, and active insulin.

- **Aerobic** (walking, running, cycling, swimming) — often lowers glucose during and after; effect can persist for hours.
- **Resistance / strength** — may lower, maintain, or temporarily raise glucose depending on intensity and insulin levels.
- **High-intensity / HIIT / sprinting** — can raise glucose via adrenaline; delayed hypoglycaemia remains possible.

### Glucose before exercise (general guidance)

| Starting glucose | General guidance |
| --- | --- |
| Below 90 mg/dL | Increased hypo risk; carbohydrate may be needed before exercise |
| 90–125 mg/dL | Some aerobic activities require preventive carbohydrate |
| 126–180 mg/dL | Often a suitable starting range, depending on insulin on board |
| 181–250 mg/dL | Consider trend, insulin activity and exercise intensity |
| Above 250 mg/dL | Check ketones, especially when glucose is unexpectedly high |

These are general considerations, not fixed rules.

The most important exercise variable is **insulin on board**. Two sessions starting at 135 mg/dL may have very different hypoglycaemia risks if one begins shortly after a meal bolus.

### Delayed hypoglycaemia

Exercise can increase insulin sensitivity for several hours and sometimes into the following night. Record glucose before, during (when practical), immediately after, and several hours later. Evening exercise deserves particular attention because lows may occur during sleep.

Avoid exercising with significant ketones or suspected insulin deficiency.

## 6. Sleep and overnight glucose

Poor sleep can increase insulin resistance and make glucose management harder the following day.

Aim for approximately **7–9 hours** with consistent sleeping and waking times.

### Investigating overnight patterns

| Pattern | Potential explanation |
| --- | --- |
| Rise from ~100 to ~170 mg/dL toward morning | Dawn phenomenon or insufficient overnight coverage |
| Fall from ~130 to ~65 mg/dL overnight | Insulin effect, exercise, alcohol, or previous bolus |
| Stays above ~200 mg/dL all night | Evening meal, insulin delivery, basal coverage, illness |
| Rise after a poor night's sleep | Increased insulin resistance or stress response |

### Dawn phenomenon

A rise during early morning, often hormonal. Examine CGM between approximately **03:00 and 08:00** over several nights. If glucose is stable until early morning then repeatedly rises without food, dawn phenomenon may contribute.

Do not assume every morning high is caused by insufficient basal insulin.

---

## 7. Stress and emotional health

Stress can raise glucose via cortisol and adrenaline (increased glucose production and reduced insulin sensitivity).

Common triggers: demanding workdays, anxiety, poor sleep, pain, illness, intense exercise.

Compare days with similar food, insulin and activity but different stress. A simple **1–5 stress rating** can help.

Avoid permanent insulin adjustments based solely on temporary stressful events without reviewing the wider pattern.

---

## 8. Alcohol, illness, hydration and daily life

### Alcohol

Can cause delayed hypoglycaemia, including overnight, by impairing hepatic glucose release. Sugary drinks may raise glucose first, then lower it later. Monitor glucose, avoid drinking on an empty stomach, and take extra overnight precautions.

### Illness

Can increase insulin requirements even when food intake falls. Follow prescribed sick-day rules, continue basal insulin, monitor more frequently, and check ketones when appropriate.

### Hydration

Drink enough fluids, especially in heat, exercise and illness. Dehydration can accompany high glucose, but water cannot replace insulin when hyperglycaemia is due to insulin deficiency.

### Other factors

Medication changes (especially steroids), travel, routine changes, injection-site problems, hormonal fluctuations, and large activity changes all influence control.

## 9. Glucose troubleshooting reference

| Glucose pattern | Possible causes | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| >180 mg/dL shortly after eating | Carb estimate, insulin timing | Meal and bolus timestamps |
| >180 mg/dL 3–6 hours after eating | Fat, protein, delayed absorption | Meal composition |
| <70 mg/dL after eating | Excess meal insulin, activity, overestimated carbs | ICR and active insulin |
| >180 mg/dL on waking | Dawn phenomenon, evening meal, basal coverage | Overnight CGM |
| <70 mg/dL overnight | Basal effect, evening exercise, alcohol | Previous 6–12 hours |
| >180 mg/dL during intense exercise | Adrenaline, insulin deficiency | Exercise intensity, ketones |
| <70 mg/dL during walking | Active insulin, muscle glucose uptake | Recent bolus and duration |
| >250 mg/dL despite corrections | Illness, delivery failure, insufficient insulin | Ketones, insulin delivery, sick-day plan |
| Unpredictable fluctuations | Injection sites, inconsistent meals/timing | Repeated patterns across days |

### Essential safety rules

**Hypoglycaemia (<70 mg/dL):** A common adult treatment is 15–20 g fast-acting carbohydrate. Recheck after 15 minutes and repeat if still low, per the individual hypo plan.

Severe confusion, seizures, unconsciousness or inability to swallow safely require emergency assistance (**999 in the UK**) and prescribed glucagon if available. Do not give food or drink by mouth if swallowing is unsafe.

**Hyperglycaemia (especially ≥250 mg/dL):** Check blood ketones when indicated, especially if ill or glucose stays high despite insulin. DKA can also occur at lower glucose.

- Blood ketones **1.6–3.0 mmol/L** → urgent diabetes-team advice.
- Ketones **≥3.0 mmol/L**, or DKA symptoms (vomiting, abdominal pain, deep breathing, confusion) → emergency assessment.

Ketones are measured in **mmol/L** even when glucose is in mg/dL.

## 10. Build a glucose intelligence system

Combine CGM with food, insulin, exercise, sleep and stress.

| Data | What to record | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CGM | Glucose, timestamp, trend | Identify excursions |
| Insulin | Units, type, injection time | Explain insulin exposure |
| Food | Carbs, meal time, fat/protein | Understand meal responses |
| Exercise | Type, duration, intensity | Immediate and delayed effects |
| Sleep | Duration, quality | Morning and overnight patterns |
| Stress | 1–5 rating | Stress-related changes |
| Illness / alcohol | Timing and severity | Explain unusual days |

### Example: investigating a post-meal spike

Illustrative dinner: before 117 mg/dL → 2 h 230 mg/dL → 4 h 205 mg/dL.  
Context: pizza 19:30, insulin 19:30, no exercise.

Possible explanation: carbs initially outpaced insulin, then delayed absorption from fat/protein.  
Next: compare similar meals, review carb estimates, discuss bolus timing with the diabetes team.

## 11. Weekly glucose review

- Review Time in Range (70–180 mg/dL)
- Review time below 70 and below 54 mg/dL
- Identify the three most common glucose excursions
- Examine overnight glucose patterns
- Compare glucose after breakfast, lunch and dinner
- Investigate exercise-related fluctuations
- Review insulin timing and injection sites
- Choose one recurring pattern to investigate next week

---

## 12. A 30-day improvement plan

| Week | Focus |
| --- | --- |
| 01 | Establish baseline — TIR, time below range, variability, recurring excursions; prioritise preventing lows |
| 02 | Nutrition and insulin — carb estimates, meal composition, bolus timing, injection technique |
| 03 | Exercise and sleep — workout changes, delayed lows, dawn phenomenon, overnight trends |
| 04 | Evaluate and personalise — compare to baseline; discuss recurring patterns and any treatment changes with the diabetes team |

---

## 13. What to prioritise

Highest-impact areas are usually:

1. Insulin delivery and timing  
2. Carbohydrate estimation  
3. Exercise-related changes  
4. Overnight glucose patterns  

Then sleep, stress, meal composition, and finer behavioural tracking for residual fluctuations.

If already at ~60–70% TIR, the next aim may be consistently **>70% TIR without increasing time <70 mg/dL**.

Hybrid closed-loop systems, smart pens, and structured CGM analysis may improve control and reduce decision burden.

## Central principle

Don't simply react to an unexpected glucose reading. First ensure safety, then investigate timing, insulin, food, exercise and surrounding circumstances. **Repeated patterns** are what allow safer, informed improvements with the diabetes team.
